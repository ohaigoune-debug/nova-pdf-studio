Modals of deduction in the past

Goal: To make logical guesses about past events using must have, may / might / could have and can't / couldn't have, and to choose the modal that matches the degree of certainty.

The idea in two lines: When we are not sure what happened in the past, we deduce from evidence. The modal shows how certain we are, and it is always followed by have + past participle.

🔵 Rules and concepts

  1. Structure: subject + modal + have + past participle: They must have used simple tools.
  2. Must have = strong positive deduction based on clear evidence.
  3. May / might / could have = a possibility; we are not sure.
  4. Can't / couldn't have = strong negative deduction; it seems impossible.
  5. The opposite of must have in deduction is can't have, not mustn't have.
  6. Passive form: modal + have been + past participle: The temple may have been destroyed by an earthquake.

🟢 Examples

  1. Evidence: the city's walls were huge and thick. Solution: The inhabitants must have feared invasions. Explanation: strong evidence, so must have.
  2. Rewrite: It is impossible that one man built this monument alone. Solution: One man can't have built this monument alone.
  3. Rewrite: Perhaps the city was abandoned because of drought. Solution: The city might have been abandoned because of drought.

🟠 Common BAC mistakes

  • Writing must have build or must had built; the correct form is must have built.
  • Using mustn't have for impossibility instead of can't have.
  • Confusing deduction (must have gone) with past obligation (had to go).
  • Choosing must have when the text gives no strong evidence.

🟡 Quick review

  • Sure yes: must have + V3.
  • Maybe: may / might / could have + V3.
  • Sure no: can't / couldn't have + V3.
  • Passive: modal + have been + V3.
